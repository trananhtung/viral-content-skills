# Viral Content Creator

Turn a raw idea into content that people understand, remember, share, and act on. This skill applies two evidence-based frameworks together:

- **SUCCESs** (Made to Stick, Chip & Dan Heath) makes an idea *stick*: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories.
- **STEPPS** (Contagious, Jonah Berger) makes it *spread*: Social Currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical Value, Stories.

Sticky but unshared content dies quietly; shareable but unmemorable content is noise. Good content needs both, so run every draft through both lenses.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Understand the brief

Establish before writing:

- **Core idea** — what is the message about?
- **Audience** — who must care? What do they already know, want, fear?
- **Format & platform** — tweet/X thread, LinkedIn post, TikTok/Reels script, blog post, email, landing page, ad, presentation…
- **Goal** — awareness (reach/shares), memory (brand/message recall), or action (click, buy, sign up, change behavior)?
- **Voice constraints** — brand tone, taboos, length limits.
- **Desired response & barrier** — what should the audience *do*, and what stops them today?
- **Delivery context** — where and when will this be seen, and can the audience act there?

Then take an **evidence inventory**: the facts, numbers, named people, real events, and proof you
actually have — and the gaps where you have none.

**Never invent factual support** — no fabricated testimonials, customers, reviews, star ratings,
deadlines, guarantees, or performance claims, in the content or in the alternative hooks. Where the
brief lacks a real story, use an explicitly hypothetical scene, a demonstration the reader performs,
or bracketed placeholders (`[named customer]`) and flag the draft as needing verification.

For other missing details, make sensible assumptions, record them in the assumptions line of the output, and proceed. Only ask questions when the answer would genuinely change the content's direction.

### Step 2: Load the frameworks

Read both reference files now, before you settle the core — they contain the detailed tactics, psychology, and examples you will draw from:

- `references/made-to-stick-success.md` — how to make the core idea stick (SUCCESs)
- `references/contagious-stepps.md` — how to make it spread (STEPPS)

Short on time, read your goal's sections first: awareness → Unexpected, Social Currency, Emotion;
recall → Simple, Concrete, Triggers, Stories; action → Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Practical Value.

### Step 3: Find the core, then choose a structure

The number one failure mode is the **Curse of Knowledge**: you know the topic so well that you communicate in abstractions the audience can't feel. Fight it with forced prioritization:

1. Write the single most important idea in one plain sentence — the "Commander's Intent". If the audience remembers only this, the content succeeded. A good core doesn't just get remembered; it lets the audience draw the right inference or make the decision you want.
2. If you have three main points, you have none. Cut until one survives.

Then choose the **opening structure**. The opening must either state the core or raise the uncertainty
the core resolves — the question may be implied rather than asked. Three legitimate structures:

- **Direct** — lead with the core or the most surprising concrete fact; order the body
  inverted-pyramid. Default for feeds, ads, and email subject lines.
- **Curiosity gap / mystery** — open the gap early, pay it off later. Legitimate for long-form,
  scripts, and talks; the Heaths' Saturn-rings mystery runs twenty pages before the answer.
- **Demonstration** — hand the audience a test they perform themselves, which reveals the core.

Supply only the context needed to understand the core or feel its gap. An audience that knows too
little to sense a gap needs orientation first; that is not throat-clearing. A complete piece must
deliver the answer it promised; a fragment may bridge toward it.

### Step 4: Engineer the content

Berger is explicit that STEPPS is a checklist, not a recipe: the principles are relatively
independent and one strong lever can carry a piece. Pick the levers that serve *this* goal and brief —
3–5 worked hard is a planning guide, not a quota — and skip what the brief has no room for. By goal:

| Goal | Usually leans on |
|------|------------|
| Awareness / shares | Social Currency, Emotion, Practical Value, Unexpected |
| Memory / recall | Simple, Concrete, Triggers, Stories |
| Action / conversion | Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Practical Value |

Engineering moves:

1. **Hook per the structure you chose in Step 2.** Whatever the structure, the surprise must be
   "post-dictable" — once revealed it obviously serves the core, rather than being a gimmick the body
   never cashes.
2. **Make the sharer look good** (Social Currency). Would passing this along make someone seem smart, in-the-know, or generous? Find the inner remarkability in the idea; give people an insider fact or a status marker.
3. **Attach a trigger.** Three things decide whether one works: how often the cue recurs for this
   audience, how *strongly* it links to your message (an overloaded cue triggers nothing), and whether
   it fires where and when the desired response is possible — the bath-mat ad that only comes to mind
   in the shower is wasted. Top of mind → tip of tongue.
4. **Choose the emotion deliberately.** High-arousal feelings — awe, amusement, excitement, anger,
   anxiety — drive sharing; sadness and mere contentment suppress it. Arousal is not mandatory, though:
   genuinely useful content spreads without it (hikers on a trail discussing vacuum cleaners). Reach
   the feeling through individual stakes, WIIFY ("what's in it for you"), identity ("people like you do
   things like this"), or a motive above money and security — pick what fits, don't stack all four.
5. **Be concrete.** Sensory nouns, specific numbers on a human scale, real people with names. Replace every abstraction with something you can picture. Use schemas and analogies ("X is Y for Z") to teach fast.
6. **Earn credibility.** Vivid specific details, statistics rescaled to human terms, truthful lived
   experience or anti-authority testimony (the dying smoker beats the Surgeon General), a testable
   claim, or one flagship proof passing the Sinatra test. State exactly what a demonstration proves:
   feeling the stitching proves it is raised, not that it survives ten years of washing.
7. **Deliver practical value.** Package genuinely useful advice, information, or a real deal so sharing it is an act of helping a friend. Narrow and specific beats broad and generic.
8. **Use a story when it does work.** Stories do two jobs: **simulation** (the audience mentally
   rehearses the behavior you want) and **inspiration** (they feel moved to act). For inspiration,
   Challenge, Connection, and Creativity plots produce different effects — match the plot to the action
   you want. Prefer found stories over invented ones; where none exists, use a hypothetical scene or a
   demonstration, labelled as such. If a brand or benefit must survive retelling, make it explain the
   story's distinctive event or payoff — adding the name to a sentence does not integrate it.
9. **Make it public / visible** (when the format allows). Design behavioral residue — something that keeps advertising after the moment passes: a hashtag identity, a template others copy, a visible artifact. What matters is an observable behavior *others could imitate*, not mere visibility of the problem.

**Handling numbers.** For empathy-led appeals, lead with one truthful individual or situation and
carry only the numbers the reader needs to understand, believe, or act — piled-on aggregate statistics
flip people into analytical mode and measurably suppress giving. A human-scale numerical *relationship*
can itself be the hook (nuclear warheads as BBs dropped in a bucket). When you round a figure for
stickiness, mark it approximate and keep the exact one available; never drop a qualification that
changes what the claim means.

**Behavior-change content.** Never present the unwanted conduct as widespread or normal among the
audience's peers — anti-drug ads that advertised how many teens use drugs increased use. Emphasize what
people *should* do; claim others already do it only with evidence. Put the reminder where and when the
next desired action is possible.

### Step 5: Draft with format discipline

- **Opening:** execute the structure chosen in Step 2. No throat-clearing — but orientation the
  audience genuinely needs is not throat-clearing.
- **Body:** one core idea, concrete language, shortest path.
- **Close:** one call to action, or the payoff that closes the gap you opened. One CTA, not three. A
  commissioned fragment closes according to its role — a bridge into the next section is a valid close.
- Respect platform norms: line breaks and skimmability for LinkedIn, brevity for X, spoken-word rhythm and visual cues for video scripts, subject line = hook for email.
- **Length:** platform limits apply to the content block, not to the assumptions line or the "why this works" receipt. State what you counted and against which limit. For video, count spoken words per timed
  segment and allow for pauses, demonstrations, and time to read on-screen text; ~2.5–3 words/second
  is a starting estimate to verify by read-through, not a rule.
- **Multiple deliverables in one brief:** give each its own content block and its own hook variants,
  with one shared "why this works".

### Step 6: Self-check, then revise once

Check only what applies to this goal and this deliverable, then fix what fails and stop — one revision
pass, not endless polishing:

- Can a stranger state the core after one read, either directly or from the resolved gap?
- Can the audience take the intended next step where and when they'll see this?
- If a story carries a brand, benefit, or idea you need remembered, does a one-sentence retelling still
  contain it?
- Does anything present unwanted behavior as normal, assert a fact the evidence inventory doesn't
  support, or exceed the format's limits?
- Is anything abstract that could be concrete? Any jargon only an insider decodes? (Curse of Knowledge)
- Goal-specific: for shares, would passing this on make the sharer look good? For recall, what cue
  brings it back? For action, what makes it believable enough to act on?

**If the draft feels flat** under a restrained brand voice, try a truthful consequence, an unresolved
tension, amusement, or awe the voice can carry. Never convert a possible outcome into a promised one to
raise the temperature.

**The evaluator loop is bounded.** If `viral-content-evaluator` is available and the user wants a
rigorous score, hand it the brief, the evidence inventory, and the draft — not your "why this works"
rationale, which argues the case the evaluator should test independently. Apply once the fixes it
supports that stay inside the brief and the evidence inventory; optionally re-evaluate once; then stop
and report what is unresolved. A score never authorizes inventing evidence.

## Output format

Deliver in this structure:

```
## [Content title / format]

[Assumptions and unverified placeholders, if any — keep brief, but always say why supplied data was discarded]

[THE CONTENT — ready to publish, formatted for the platform]

---
**Why this works:** [3-5 bullets mapping specific choices to principles, e.g.
"Hook opens a curiosity gap (Unexpected) with a concrete number (Concrete)"]

**Alternative hooks:**
1. [variant]
2. [variant]
```

Keep the "why" section short — the content is the deliverable, the analysis is the receipt.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Three messages in one piece (= zero messages), or a feature list where one story would do.
- A clever surprise unrelated to the core (gimmick), or a remarkable fact buried in paragraph four.
- Corporate self-congratulation ("we're proud to announce…") where the goal needed activation.
- Abstract virtue words — "innovative", "world-class", "unique" — instead of evidence (semantic stretch).
- Buzz without benefit: a story people retell with your brand edited out of it.
- Optimizing only for shares when the goal is conversion, or vice versa.
