# Made to Stick — The SUCCESs Framework (Chip & Dan Heath)

A reference for creating and evaluating content and messaging, distilled from *Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die* by Chip Heath and Dan Heath.
An idea is "sticky" when it is understood, remembered, and changes how people think or behave. The Heaths reverse-engineered naturally sticky ideas (urban legends, proverbs, great ads, memorable lessons) and found six recurring traits, forming the checklist **SUCCESs**: **Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories**. The villain of every principle is the **Curse of Knowledge**: once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it. In Elizabeth Newton's tappers-and-listeners experiment, people tapping a well-known song predicted listeners would guess it 50% of the time; listeners actually got 2.5%. The tapper hears the melody in her head; the listener hears disconnected taps. Experts communicate the same way — in abstractions, jargon, and summaries that carry a tune only they can hear. The checklist exists to force you back into the listener's world.

## 1. Simple

**What it is.**
Find the core of your idea and express it compactly. Simple = core + compact — not dumbed down, not a sound bite, but a single prioritized message stripped of everything that is merely important, so the *most* important thing stands alone. The ideal form is a proverb: short enough to remember, deep enough to guide a lifetime of decisions.

**Why it works.**
People make decisions under uncertainty and overload, and choice itself paralyzes (Tversky/Shafir: adding a second good option made students *less* likely to pick either). A single clear priority rescues them from decision paralysis and lets thousands of people improvise consistently without instructions. Saint-Exupéry's test: perfection is when there is nothing left to take away.

**Key mechanisms / tactics:**
- **Find the core (Commander's Intent).** The US Army learned "no plan survives contact with the enemy," so every order leads with one plain-language statement of the goal. If everything else fails, the intent still directs behavior. Ask: "If we do nothing else tomorrow, we must ___."
- **Forced prioritization.** A trial lawyer: argue ten points, even good ones, and the jury remembers none. Clinton's advisers to their policy-wonk candidate: "If you say three things, you say nothing." One North Star, not five.
- **Inverted pyramid — don't bury the lede.** Journalists put the most essential fact in the first sentence; readers keep the most no matter where they stop. The classic failure ("burying the lede") comes from being so deep in details you lose the story.
- **Proverbs as the ideal.** "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" has survived 2,500+ years across dozens of languages with zero marketing budget — compact AND core. Compact without core is just a slogan; avoid empty catchphrases.
- **Tap existing schemas.** Memory works by pointers to what people already know (JFK-FBI-NATO chunking). Define the new in terms of the known: a pomelo is "a supersized grapefruit with a thick, soft rind" — one sentence does a paragraph's work and lets listeners make correct inferences.
- **High-concept pitches.** Hollywood greenlights $100M on one analogy: *Speed* = "Die Hard on a bus"; *Alien* = "Jaws on a spaceship." A compact analogy loads enormous coordinated meaning into a few words.
- **Generative analogy.** Disney calls employees "cast members": they audition, wear costumes, are always "on stage." The metaphor generates correct behavior in situations no manual covers. (Contrast Subway's inert "sandwich artists.") Accessible-but-imperfect beats precise-but-opaque: a message that can't guide a decision is useless however accurate.
- **Layer simplicity into complexity.** Teach the simplified model first (planetary atom, two-person trade economy), then extend. Give just enough to be useful, then a bit more.

**Signature examples:**
- Southwest Airlines' single filter — "We are THE low-fare airline" — lets any employee instantly reject a nice-sounding idea (the chicken Caesar salad).
- Carville's whiteboard for the 1992 campaign: "It's the economy, stupid."
- Publisher Hoover Adams' local-paper proverb "Names, names, and names" governed thousands of issues without his presence.
- Jeff Hawkins fought feature creep by carrying a wooden block the size of the Palm Pilot — a visual proverb: do four things well.
- The sun-exposure rewrite: lead with "skin damage is cumulative and irreversible," not tanning-as-status-symbol trivia.

**Common mistakes:**
- Burying the lede — opening with interesting-but-irrelevant material, or ordering by discovery instead of importance.
- Weighting every point equally, drowning the audience in decision paralysis.
- Mistaking short slogans for core ideas, or refusing to cut accurate-but-useless precision ("we can't oversimplify!") and thereby communicating nothing.

**Quick check:**
1. Can you state the one thing that matters most in a single proverb-like sentence — and does the content lead with it?
2. If a reader remembers only one sentence a week from now, is it the right one?
3. Could someone use this message alone to make a decision you'd endorse?

## 2. Unexpected

**What it is.**
Get attention by breaking a pattern (surprise), and hold attention over time by creating curiosity (interest). Violate the audience's expectations about how the world works — then satisfy the itch you created.

**Why it works.**
Brains ignore the constant and lock onto change. Surprise is the emotion triggered when our "guessing machines" (schemas) fail; it forces a pause and focuses attention on repairing the failed prediction — extra attention and thought that stamps the event into memory. But surprise is brief; sustained interest requires an open question the audience wants answered.

**Key mechanisms / tactics:**
- **Break a schema tied to your core.** Identify what is genuinely counterintuitive in your message ("Why isn't this already happening naturally?") and open by breaking the audience's guessing machine at exactly that point — then help them rebuild it. Common sense is the enemy: a message that sounds like what they already believe goes in one ear and out the other.
- **Avoid gimmicky surprise — make it "post-dictable."** A twist must make sense in retrospect and serve the core. The fake word PHRAUG delights (aha — "frog"); HENSION only frustrates. A Super Bowl ad of wolves attacking a marching band was unforgettable and message-free; the same shock welded to the core (the Enclave crash ad) is powerful.
- **Curiosity gap theory (Loewenstein).** Curiosity arises when we feel a gap in our knowledge, and gaps are painful — we sit through bad movies to close them. Open gaps before closing them: pose a question or puzzle, highlight what others know that they don't, ask for a prediction, promise a resolution. Shift from "What do I need to tell them?" to "What questions do I want them to ask?"
- **Mysteries and questions.** Cialdini found the best science writing opens with a mystery (What are Saturn's rings made of? Three elite teams say gas, dust, ice — who's right?) and resolves it over twenty pages; students refused to leave when class ended before the reveal. The "Aha!" is more satisfying after a "Huh?"
- **Fight overconfidence with commitment.** People think they already know; make them commit to a guess first (Mazur's concept tests) or engineer disagreement — then the reveal lands.
- **Sequential knowledge gaps / context first.** If the audience knows too little to care (an abyss, not a gap), build background until gaps become visible — Roone Arledge made strangers care about NCAA football by showing the town, the campus, the rivalry before kickoff. Drop clues like a treasure map, not a data dump.
- **Big, long-lived gaps.** Sony's "pocket radio" and Kennedy's "man on the moon within the decade" were audacious, vivid gaps — large enough to inspire, concrete enough to feel bridgeable — that directed effort for years.

**Signature examples:**
- Flight attendant Karen Wood's joke-filled safety briefing drew applause for the world's most-ignored speech.
- The Enclave "minivan ad" ends in a sudden crash: "Didn't see that coming? No one ever does. Buckle up."
- Nordstrom instills its service standard with shocking "Nordie" stories — gift-wrapping goods bought at Macy's, warming customers' cars in winter, refunding tire chains Nordstrom never sold.
- Nora Ephron's journalism teacher let students write leads from facts about a faculty trip, then revealed the real lede — "There will be no school next Thursday" — rewriting their schema of journalism in one stroke.
- Local-news teasers ("An invisible chemical in your home may be killing you") are crude proof that knowledge gaps compel.

**Common mistakes:**
- Gimmicky, unrelated surprise — memorable but message-free (the wolves problem).
- Being merely clever instead of counterintuitive about the core; teasing gaps you never close.
- Dumping all the facts up front so there is never a gap to care about.

**Quick check:**
1. What expectation does this content violate — and is that violation the core message itself?
2. Does it open a curiosity gap early and close it, or just announce conclusions?
3. Is the surprise post-dictable — will readers say "of course!" afterward, not "huh?"

## 3. Concrete

**What it is.**
Express ideas in sensory language and specific human actions: things you can see, touch, and picture. "Concrete" is a V8 engine, an ice-filled bathtub, a Nordie ironing a shirt; "abstract" is high performance, world-class service, stakeholder value.

**Why it works.**
Velcro theory of memory: the brain is covered in loops; the more sensory hooks an idea has, the more places it attaches. Concrete words are remembered far better than abstract ones, and oral traditions preserved concrete action while abstractions evaporated. Concreteness is also the only universally shared language: experts think in abstractions (the Curse of Knowledge is exactly this drift), novices need the tangible — and even experts coordinate better on concrete ground.

**Key mechanisms / tactics:**
- **Sensory, specific language.** Aesop encoded "don't be bitter about what you can't have" as a fox and sour grapes — that encoding is why it survived 2,500 years. Write the fable, not the moral.
- **Concrete grounds coordination between experts and novices.** Bechky's chip-machine study: when a part didn't fit, engineers kept refining abstract drawings while manufacturers just wanted them down on the floor at the machine. Fix problems at the level everyone speaks. Boeing aimed the 727 not at "best airliner" but at: 131 seats, Miami–New York nonstop, land on La Guardia's runway 4-22 — and thousands of specialists coordinated.
- **Make abstractions tangible.** The Nature Conservancy stopped selling acres and named "landscapes" (the Mount Hamilton Wilderness) donors could picture and rally around. Stone Yamashita turned HP's pitch to Disney into a walk-through exhibit of the fictional Ferraris family's Disney World trip — abstraction became a family photo from a roller coaster. A 73-year-old dancer, Thomas Dwyer, proved "diversity" better than any values statement.
- **Teach through examples in context, not abstractions.** Asian math classes outperform partly by "computing in context" (you have 100 yen, buy a 70-yen notebook) and physically visible operations (rows of tiles removed = subtraction). A semester-long accounting soap opera (helping fictional students Kris and Sandy launch a startup) had students *discovering* fixed vs. variable costs — with grade gains still measurable two years later.
- **Concrete props mobilize knowledge.** People can list nearly as many "white things in your refrigerator" as "white things" — specificity focuses the mind. Jerry Kaplan tossed a maroon leather portfolio onto Kleiner Perkins' table as "a model of the future of computing" and turned an interrogation into a brainstorm.
- **Put a face on the audience.** Hamburger Helper's team visited real moms' kitchens ("Fingertips"), learned flavors-not-shapes, and drove a line simplification and an 11% sales rebound. Saddleback Church designs everything for "Saddleback Sam," a specific skeptical suburban professional. Trader Joe's serves "an unemployed college professor who drives a very used Volvo."

**Signature examples:**
- Jane Elliott's blue-eyes/brown-eyes exercise made prejudice a lived, sensory experience — collar necklaces, sudden cruelty, plummeting test scores — and her third-graders were measurably less prejudiced decades later.
- James Grant of UNICEF carried a packet of salt and sugar (Oral Rehydration Therapy) and told prime ministers: this costs less than a cup of tea and can save hundreds of thousands of your children — a prop that beat pages of epidemiology.
- The kidney-heist legend endures on the bathtub of ice and the tube in the lower back.
- Boeing's runway 4-22 goal; Kaplan's maroon portfolio; "Saddleback Sam."

**Common mistakes:**
- Abstraction creep — mission-statement language ("synergy," "world-class," "customer-centric") that means nothing outside the expert's head.
- Explaining mechanisms and categories when one worked example would teach more.
- Instructions written like "cook to a rich consistency" instead of minutes, quantities, and pictures.

**Quick check:**
1. Could a reader close their eyes and picture what this says — is there anything to see, hold, or do?
2. Would a novice and an expert interpret every key sentence the same way?
3. Is every abstraction grounded in at least one specific person, object, number-in-context, or scene?

## 4. Credible

**What it is.**
Give people reasons to believe. Sticky ideas carry their own credentials — through authorities or anti-authorities, vivid details, human-scale statistics, one flagship proof, or a claim the audience can test themselves.

**Why it works.**
Barry Marshall and Robin Warren discovered that bacteria cause ulcers and were laughed at for a decade — truth alone doesn't persuade; belief has sources. Most communicators lack Surgeon-General authority, so the message itself must supply the proof. Marshall finally broke through by drinking a beaker of H. pylori and giving himself gastritis: an unforgettable, self-demonstrating credential.

**Key mechanisms / tactics:**
- **External authority — and anti-authority.** Experts and admired figures vouch for ideas, but honesty and lived experience often beat status: Pam Laffin, a 29-year-old mother dying of emphysema, was a more powerful anti-smoking spokesperson than any official; the Doe Fund let Dennis, a formerly homeless driver, be the pitch. Ask: whose testimony would a skeptic actually trust?
- **Internal credibility via vivid details.** In a mock custody trial, jurors rated the mother more favorably when her side's arguments carried irrelevant-but-vivid details (the son's Darth Vader toothbrush). Details make claims feel real and signal knowledge — use true, core-relevant details, not decoration.
- **Statistics on a human scale.** Numbers should illustrate a relationship, not be the takeaway. Beyond War dropped one BB in a bucket (Hiroshima), ten (one submarine's missiles), then poured in 5,000 (the world's arsenal) — no one remembers the number; everyone remembers the roar. Stephen Covey translated engagement data into a soccer team where only 4 of 11 players know which goal is theirs. Cisco reframed a $500/employee wireless network as "pays for itself if it saves each person 1–2 minutes a day." Compare honestly: deer kill ~300× more people than sharks. Use statistics as input to form your view, not decoration for a pre-made one.
- **The Sinatra Test.** "If I can make it there, I'll make it anywhere" — one example so strong it establishes credibility everywhere. India's Safexpress won a Bollywood film-distribution deal by citing two deliveries: every Harry Potter book to every bookstore by 8 a.m. on release day, and the national board-exam papers. Bill McDonough's Swiss textile mill (only 38 of 8,000 chemicals passed; inspectors thought their instruments were broken because outflow water was cleaner than the inflow; scraps became garden insulation; costs down 20%) passes the Sinatra Test for "green can be profitable."
- **Testable credentials — "try before you buy."** Let the audience verify the claim themselves. Wendy's "Where's the beef?" invited customers to compare its patty with a Big Mac's (sales +31% in a year). Reagan skipped statistics: "Are you better off than you were four years ago?" The Emotional Tank exercise let coaches discover for themselves that tearing down is easy and filling up is hard. The NBA's rookie orientation: attractive fans flirt with rookies at the hotel bar, then introduce themselves next morning — "Hi, I'm Sheila, and I'm HIV-positive." (Caution: the mechanism can be abused — the Snapple/KKK rumor "verified" itself with an innocent kosher K; a true verification step can smuggle in a false conclusion.)

**Signature examples:**
- Marshall drinking the ulcer bacteria to prove causation.
- The Darth Vader toothbrush swaying jurors.
- Nuclear warheads as BBs in a bucket; deer-vs-shark death comparison.
- Safexpress' Harry Potter delivery as a Sinatra-Test credential.
- The flesh-eating-bananas email boosting itself with fake institutional endorsements — proof people instinctively look for authority tags.

**Common mistakes:**
- Statistics without scale or context (raw billions; "37 grams of saturated fat").
- Leaning on titles and credentials the audience doesn't trust instead of demonstrations.
- Cherry-picking dramatic but dishonest comparisons — credibility dies when the trick is spotted.

**Quick check:**
1. Why would a skeptical reader believe this — what internal or external credential does the content itself carry?
2. Is every statistic translated into human-scale terms that illustrate a relationship?
3. Is there one Sinatra-Test example, or a way for the audience to verify the claim themselves?

## 5. Emotional

**What it is.**
Make people *feel* something, because feeling is what makes them care, and caring precedes action. Not tear-jerking for its own sake — finding the emotion that connects your idea to what the audience already cares about: themselves, their identity, their group.

**Why it works.**
Mother Teresa principle: "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will." In the Carnegie Mellon study, donors gave $2.38 after reading about one girl, Rokia, and $1.14 after reading famine statistics — and adding statistics to Rokia's story *cut* giving to $1.43. Even priming people with an arithmetic problem before the appeal slashed donations: the analytical hat suppresses the feeling hat. Belief isn't enough — teenagers believe smoking kills and smoke anyway.

**Key mechanisms / tactics:**
- **One individual beats statistics.** Charities sponsor a child, not "poverty in Africa." Show the person; keep numbers offstage or in support.
- **Ride existing associations — and beware semantic stretch.** Ideas borrow power by association ("relativity" invoked for awe), but overuse dilutes: "unique" rose 73% in newspapers while "unusual" fell — the word stretched until it meant nothing. When a term is exhausted (as "sportsmanship" was — its trophies a consolation prize for losers), move to fresh ground: Jim Thompson's "Honoring the Game" cut technical fouls from one per 15 games to one per 52.
- **Appeal to self-interest — WIIFY ("what's in it for you").** Caples' mail-order ads led with benefits, not features ("They laughed when I sat down at the piano…"): people buy quarter-inch holes, not quarter-inch drills. Keep "you" in the sentence. Better still, have people *visualize themselves* enjoying the benefit: Tempe homeowners asked to imagine themselves using cable TV subscribed at 47% vs. 20% for those merely told the benefits.
- **Go beyond Maslow's basement.** People predict *others* are motivated by money and security while claiming higher motives (learning, self-actualization, transcendence) for themselves — so most messaging camps in the basement and forfeits the strongest appeals. Floyd Lee, running the Pegasus mess hall in Iraq on the same 21-day Army menu as everyone else, declared "I'm in charge of morale, not food" — and soldiers drove Iraq's most dangerous roads for Sunday prime rib.
- **Identity appeals over pure self-interest.** People often decide by asking "Who am I? What kind of situation is this? What would someone like me do?" (March's model), not by calculating consequences — firefighters angrily refused a free popcorn popper for previewing a safety film; decades of polling show narrow self-interest barely predicts political opinion. "Don't Mess with Texas" used Dallas Cowboys and Willie Nelson to tell the litter-prone "Bubba" that real Texans don't litter: visible roadside litter fell 72% in five years, and the planned fear campaign was cancelled as unnecessary.
- **Ask "Why?" three times.** The Curse of Knowledge hides why *you* care. A duo-piano foundation's "preserve duo piano music" left a room cold until repeated whys produced: "the sound of an orchestra with the intimacy of chamber music." Drill from what you do to why it matters to outsiders.
- **Create empathy through experience.** IDEO filmed an ER visit from the patient's point of view (endless ceilings, faceless voices, unexplained waiting); staff said "I never realized…" and immediately wanted to fix it.

**Signature examples:**
- Rokia vs. statistics — the Save the Children donation experiment.
- The Truth campaign — teens stacking 1,800 body bags outside Big Tobacco HQ, channeling teenage rebellion *against* the manipulative industry — versus Philip Morris' analytical "Think. Don't Smoke." (Truth exposure cut intent to smoke; "Think" exposure *raised* it 36%.)
- "Don't Mess with Texas" — identity, not fines, cleaned the highways.
- Floyd Lee's Pegasus mess hall — "in charge of morale."
- "Math is weightlifting for the brain" — an algebra teacher answering "when will we use this?" with "Never… you don't lift weights so someone can put a barbell on your chest; you lift to be stronger at everything" — moving the appeal up Maslow.

**Common mistakes:**
- Appealing only to base self-interest (or fear) when identity or higher motives would bite harder.
- Piling on statistics that flip the audience into analytical mode and suppress feeling.
- Leaning on worn-out emotional words (semantic stretch) instead of finding a fresh association; assuming others care as much as you do while your "why" goes unstated.

**Quick check:**
1. Whom will the reader picture — one specific person, or a mass and a number?
2. What exactly should the reader feel, and does the content evoke it rather than assert it?
3. Does it answer WIIFY — and does it touch identity ("people like me do things like this"), not just calculation?

## 6. Stories

**What it is.**
Tell stories — accounts of specific people acting through specific events — rather than summarizing lessons. Stories drive action two ways: as **simulation** (mental rehearsal of how to act) and as **inspiration** (energy to act).

**Why it works.**
Hearing a story is not passive: audiences mentally simulate it (readers even take longer to process an object the protagonist left in another room). Mental simulation measurably builds skill and problem-solving — UCLA students who simulated the *events* around a problem coped better than those who visualized happy outcomes, and mental practice alone yields roughly two-thirds of the benefit of physical practice. A story is a flight simulator for the brain: it packages knowledge with context, emotion, and causality. It also defeats the Curse of Knowledge, because stories force concrete language. You can derive the lesson from the story, but you cannot reconstruct the story from the lesson.

**Key mechanisms / tactics:**
- **Stories as simulation.** Firefighters and nurses swap shop talk to multiply experience. Gary Klein's ICU nurse ignored a normal-looking heart monitor, listened with a stethoscope, diagnosed air compressing the newborn's heart, and saved the baby — teaching both the condition and "don't blindly trust machines." Xerox techs trading the tale of a misleading E053 error code learn more than any manual bullet could convey. Prefer a narrated incident over a tip list: a professor's blow-by-blow account of defusing a classroom bully (peer disapproval did what confrontation couldn't) beats "stay calm; don't get defensive."
- **Stories as inspiration.** Jared's story energized action in a way "7 subs under 6 grams of fat" never could.
- **Don't invent — spot.** The world produces better stories than any writer; the skill is recognizing one when it crosses your desk and matching it to your core. Nordstrom circulates Nordie service stories; FedEx tells Purple Promise stories (a driver who talked a competitor's driver into finishing her route); Costco tells "salmon stories" of relentlessly raising quality while cutting price.
- **Three inspirational plots** (cover the great majority of inspirational stories):
  - **Challenge plot** — a protagonist overcomes daunting obstacles (David vs. Goliath, Jared, Rose Blumkin, the 1980 US hockey team). Inspires perseverance and hard work; right for a project kickoff.
  - **Connection plot** — people bridge a social gap (the Good Samaritan crossing bitter ethnic lines; Mean Joe Greene and the kid with a Coke). Inspires tolerance, helping, teamwork; right for the holiday party, wrong before battle.
  - **Creativity plot** — a mental breakthrough or inventive solution (Newton's apple; Ingersoll-Rand's "Drag Test," bumper-dragging materials around a parking lot instead of months of lab studies; Shackleton bunking the complainers in his own tent). Inspires experimentation and fresh thinking.
- **Springboard stories.** Stephen Denning at the World Bank couldn't sell "knowledge management" with arguments, so he told of a health worker in Zambia who found malaria answers on a CDC website — a story that let executives imagine the change and start generating their own plans ("They stole my idea — it became *their* idea!"). Arguments invite the audience to critique; stories invite them to participate. Engage the skeptical "little voice in the head" by giving it something to do.
- **Keep the story on-core.** Stories are naturally concrete, emotional, and often unexpected; the hard part is Simple — choosing the story that embodies *your* message, and keeping the kicker attached.

**Signature examples:**
- Jared Fogle — 425 lb, size-60 waist, warned he might not see 35 — lost 245 lb on his self-invented Subway diet; spotted by franchisee Bob Ocwieja, filmed free by the agency after national marketing and lawyers said no; Subway sales rose 18% then 16%, versus the flat statistical "7 Under 6" campaign. Every SUCCESs box checked — and any checklist user could have picked the winner in advance.
- The nurse and the lying heart monitor.
- The Zambia springboard story at the World Bank.
- Conference organizers who summarized presentations as the speakers' own stories — and the speakers, cursed by knowledge, demanded their bullet-point platitudes ("keep communication channels open") back.

**Common mistakes:**
- Summarizing instead of storytelling — extracting the moral and discarding the narrative that made it learnable.
- Telling a great story that doesn't reflect the core message (a Connection plot before battle).
- Waiting for cinematic drama (even mundane stories beat bullet points) or inventing/embellishing when honest spotting would do.

**Quick check:**
1. Is there an actual narrative — a specific person, obstacle, and resolution — or only conclusions and tips?
2. Which plot is it (Challenge / Connection / Creativity), and does that plot's effect match the action you want?
3. Could the audience mentally rehearse doing the right thing from this story alone?

## Using SUCCESs

The checklist costs nothing and requires no creative genius — the Heaths' central promise is that ordinary people (a nutrition activist, a journalism teacher, a mess-hall manager, a sandwich franchisee, a schoolteacher) made ideas stick by applying these traits, usually without knowing their names. Systematic beats inspired: novices trained for two hours on creativity templates out-produced both untrained peers and classic free-association brainstormers. What the checklist demands instead of genius is **discipline and awareness**, because at every step the enemy is the **Curse of Knowledge**: you will drift into Expert-speak; the checklist is your translation back into the universal language of the concrete.

Map audience needs to the checklist — and don't trust your own cursed judgment ("of course they'll understand/care"); ask the concrete question instead:

| The audience must… | Ask… |
|---|---|
| Pay attention | Is it **Unexpected**? (break a schema; open a curiosity gap) |
| Understand & remember | Is it **Concrete**? (sensory, specific, example-driven) |
| Believe / agree | Is it **Credible**? (details, human-scale stats, Sinatra test, testable credential) |
| Care | Is it **Emotional**? (one person, identity, WIIFY, above the basement) |
| Act | Is it a **Story**? (simulation + inspiration; springboard) |

**Simple** runs underneath all five: it is the Answer-stage work of deciding what to say — the core — and compacting it so everything else has a spine. Communication has two stages: reaching the Answer (where expertise helps) and Telling Others (where the same expertise curses you); most training covers only the first.

**Villains recap — diagnose these first when content isn't landing:**
- **The Curse of Knowledge** (master villain): abstractions, jargon, and conclusions delivered because you can't recall not-knowing. Antidotes: concrete language, stories, analogies, asking "why?" three times.
- **Buried ledes:** the most important point hidden mid-document; information ordered by discovery, not importance.
- **Decision paralysis:** too many points, options, or co-equal priorities; no forced prioritization, so the audience nods and does nothing.
- **Presentation over message:** polish and charisma can't save an unfocused message — in the Stanford one-minute-speech exercise, delivery skill didn't predict recall at all; ten minutes later, 63% of listeners remembered the stories and only 5% remembered any individual statistic (speakers had averaged 2.5 statistics per talk; only 1 in 10 told a story).
- **Semantic stretch and clichéd emotion:** exhausted words and borrowed associations that no longer make anyone feel anything.

Two closing disciplines:
1. **The audience gets a vote.** They will compress, mutate, and select from your message (Durocher's "nice guys finish last," Holmes' never-said "Elementary, my dear Watson," Carville's one surviving phrase of three). Judge success by whether the mutated version keeps your core, not by verbatim fidelity.
2. **Fight sticky with stickier.** You can't "unstick" a false idea with denials ("completely unfounded" never beats "worms in the burger"). Counter with a stickier idea — Ray Kroc's "hamburger costs $1.50 a pound; worms cost $6," the Goodtimes-virus parody that killed hoax warnings by making them laughable — or with a demonstrable action, like the early auto industry's reliability races that let thousands *see* that you could, in fact, sit on an explosion.
