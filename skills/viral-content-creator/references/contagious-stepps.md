# Contagious: The STEPPS Framework — Practical Reference

Distilled from Jonah Berger's *Contagious: Why Things Catch On*. Use this file when creating or evaluating marketing and social content.

**Core thesis:** Word of mouth is the primary engine of popularity — it drives 20–50% of purchase decisions and is at least 10x more effective than traditional advertising, because it is both more credible (friends tell you the truth; ads always claim to be the best) and better targeted (people share with exactly the person the information fits). Counterintuitively, only about **7% of word of mouth happens online** — most sharing is face-to-face, so don't equate "viral" with social media; Facebook and Twitter are technologies, not strategies. The **message matters more than the messenger**: contagious content spreads regardless of whether the sharer is an "influencer," just as a truly funny joke lands no matter who tells it (social epidemics are forest fires — size depends on the fuel, not the spark). And **virality isn't luck or an innate property** — it is engineered. A blender company made grinding marbles into a 300M-view phenomenon; a $100 cheesesteak made a new Philadelphia restaurant famous. Any product, idea, or behavior can be designed to be talked about. Berger's research (New York Times most-emailed lists, BzzAgent campaigns, brand-mention data, baby names, car sales) distills the recipe into six principles: **STEPPS** — Social Currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical Value, Stories.

## 1. Social Currency

**What it is.** People share things that make them look smart, cool, in-the-know, or interesting — talk is a currency for buying good impressions, like the car we drive or the clothes we wear. Give people a way to look good while talking about your product and they will spread it for you.

**Why it works.** Self-disclosure is intrinsically rewarding: brain-imaging studies show sharing our own opinions activates the same reward circuits as food and money, and people will even accept less pay for the chance to share. What we mention signals identity, so we curate: we tell the story that makes us look sharp and skip the one that makes us look dull — and retellings drift toward the more remarkable version (the fish grows with each telling).

**Key mechanisms / tactics**
- **Inner remarkability:** find the surprising, novel, or extreme angle hidden in any product — even a boring one. Break the expected pattern (a $100 cheesesteak, a blender that pulverizes an iPhone, black toilet paper at a party, JetBlue perks on a budget airline). Mystery and controversy also fuel talk (The Blair Witch Project).
- **Game mechanics & status:** points, levels, tiers, badges, and leaderboards let people earn and display achievement. Status is relative — people prefer earning less if it means outperforming peers — so visible hierarchies drive both effort and bragging (which mentions the brand). Make status systems simple enough for outsiders to read (gold/silver/bronze beats platinum-vs-sapphire confusion). Contests, awards, and popular-vote competitions make entrants market you while campaigning for themselves.
- **Scarcity & exclusivity — make people feel like insiders:** hard-to-get things seem more valuable, and insiders talk to show they're in the know. Secrets are especially contagious ("please don't tell" practically guarantees telling). Use scarcity of time, quantity, or access; a useful pattern is restrict first, relax later. Soften refusals with "no, but…" so exclusivity doesn't read as snobbery.
- **Prefer social incentives over cash:** paying people for referrals crowds out intrinsic motivation; status and insider feelings keep working for free (fantasy football devotion runs on bragging rights, not money).

**Signature examples**
- *Please Don't Tell* — a secret NYC bar entered through a phone booth in a hot dog shop; no advertising, yet the hardest reservation in town, spread purely by insiders sharing the secret (and handed a card with the phone number on exit).
- *Barclay Prime's $100 cheesesteak* — Kobe beef, lobster, and champagne turned a $5 local staple into national news and made a new steakhouse thrive.
- *Snapple facts* — surprising trivia under bottle caps (kangaroos can't walk backwards) that people can't help repeating.
- *Frequent-flyer status tiers* — flying became a game; the author paid more for a layover route just to cross a status threshold; miles are hoarded (under 10% redeemed) because the game itself is the reward.
- *Rue La La* — invite-only flash sales sold the same goods as struggling SmartBargains but boomed because members felt like insiders; *McRib* — McDonald's revived a mediocre sandwich purely by making it scarce and rotating cities; *Foursquare badges* and *Burberry's "Art of the Trench"* turned customers into self-promoters.

**Common mistakes**
- Talking about why the company is great instead of giving the *audience* a way to look great.
- Paying for buzz (cash referral bonuses) instead of designing social rewards — money crowds out authentic advocacy.
- Status systems or exclusivity too confusing or too harsh: unreadable tier names, or so much "no" that customers walk.

**Quick check**
- If someone shares this, do they look smarter, cooler, or more in-the-know for doing so?
- Is there a surprising fact or pattern-break in the content, or is it merely informative?
- Does anything let the audience feel like insiders (access, scarcity, achievement they can display)?

## 2. Triggers

**What it is.** Triggers are environmental cues that make people think of a product or idea — peanut butter reminds us of jelly. People talk about whatever is on their mind, so the more often something is cued by daily life, the more it gets mentioned, chosen, and bought. "Top of mind means tip of tongue."

**Why it works.** Most conversation is casual filler, not performance — we talk about whatever's mentally accessible. Accessible thoughts drive behavior directly: French music playing in a store sells French wine; students shown a slogan tied to cafeteria trays ate 25% more fruit and vegetables (even though they found the slogan corny); voters assigned to vote *in schools* passed a school-funding measure at measurably higher rates. Crucially, Berger's BzzAgent research found "interesting" products get **no more total word of mouth** than boring ones — interesting earns *immediate* buzz, but only frequent triggers sustain *ongoing* buzz (Cheerios is tweeted about more than Disney World, spiking every breakfast time).

**Key mechanisms / tactics**
- **Link to frequent cues:** attach the product to something people encounter daily. Frequency beats fit — coffee triggers Kit Kat better than hot chocolate because coffee is everywhere year-round; "Weekends are for Michelob" worked where "Holidays…" failed.
- **Grow the habitat:** products live in a habitat of cues; deliberately create new associations through repeated pairing (Kit Kat + coffee; Boston Market + dinner, which lifted word of mouth 20% among lunch-only customers). You can even parasitize a rival's ads so their message triggers yours ("poison parasite" anti-smoking spoofs of Marlboro).
- **Trigger near the point of action:** the cue must fire where and when the behavior can happen. An anti-soda ad showing fat pouring from the can triggers at drinking time (effective); a memorable bath-mat safety ad triggers in the shower where you can't buy one (wasted). Reusable grocery bags need a cue at home (the shopping list), not the parking lot.
- **Prefer strong triggers over clever slogans:** a boring but frequently cued product (Ziploc bags, moisturizer) out-talks a witty one-day novelty. GEICO's cavemen were funny but nothing in daily life cues cavemen; Budweiser's "Wassup?" rode an everyday greeting.
- **Context is local and temporal:** cheesesteaks trigger daily in Philadelphia, rarely in Chicago; orange products spike before Halloween and vanish after. Even negative attention helps unknown products if it keeps them cued (bad reviews *raised* sales ~45% for unknown authors; "smells like sweaty socks" boosted a wine).

**Signature examples**
- *Rebecca Black's "Friday"* — a famously bad song whose searches spiked every single Friday: the calendar itself was the trigger.
- *Kit Kat + coffee* — "a break's best friend" pairing revived a fading brand, sales up a third; brand value grew from ~$300M toward $500M.
- *Mars bar sales rose during NASA's Pathfinder mission* — massive media coverage of the planet Mars triggered the candy bar (named after Franklin Mars, not the planet).
- *Cheerios vs. Disney World* — the "boring" cereal earns more word of mouth because breakfast happens every day; Disney trips happen once a year.
- *Voting location effects* and the *cafeteria-tray slogan* — cues change real behavior, not just talk.

**Common mistakes**
- Optimizing for clever/funny/interesting while ignoring whether anything in daily life will ever cue the message again.
- Choosing a rare or overloaded cue: infrequent events (holidays, cavemen) fire rarely; ubiquitous cues linked to everything (the color red) are too diluted to own.
- Placing the trigger far from the moment of action (message seen at the clinic, behavior needed at the bar).

**Quick check**
- What, specifically, in the audience's daily environment will remind them of this — and how often does that cue occur?
- Does the reminder fire at a time and place where they can actually act (share, buy, vote)?
- Is this built for ongoing word of mouth or just a one-day spike?

## 3. Emotion

**What it is.** When we care, we share. Content that makes people *feel* something gets passed along; content that merely informs does not. But the driver is not positivity — it's physiological **arousal**: the activated, ready-to-act state some emotions produce.

**Why it works.** In Berger's analysis of ~7,000 New York Times articles, awe-inspiring pieces were ~30% more likely to make the most-emailed list — and anger and anxiety (negative but high-arousal) also *increased* sharing, while sadness (negative, low-arousal) *decreased* it by ~16%. Positive vs. negative isn't the axis; activation is. High-arousal emotions — **awe, excitement, amusement/humor, anger, anxiety** (and even disgust) — kindle the fire and push people to act and transmit; low-arousal states — **sadness, contentment** — power people down and suppress sharing. Arousal from *any* source spreads to sharing: people made to jog in place for 60 seconds were about twice as likely to email an unrelated article afterward.

**Key mechanisms / tactics**
- **Focus on feelings, not function:** don't lead with features and facts — information alone rarely changes behavior (smokers know smoking is bad). Use the "Three Whys" (from Made to Stick): repeatedly ask why the thing matters until you hit the emotional core (search → answers → connecting with people, achieving dreams — that's what Google's "Parisian Love" dramatized).
- **Pick high-arousal emotions:** inspire awe, spark excitement, make people laugh — or, on the negative side, make them angry or anxious rather than sad. Berger's experiments: making a story more anger-inducing or an ad funnier directly increased sharing.
- **Negative emotion can work:** BMW's dark "The Hire" films (kidnappings, near-death) drew 11M views and sales rose 12%; NYC's disgusting "Man Drinks Fat" anti-soda ad spread widely. Used deliberately, fear/anger/disgust beat bland positivity.
- **Mind the backfire:** high-arousal negativity spreads *against* you too — monitor for anger early ("pissed off," "mad") and defuse before it snowballs (the Motrin baby-wearing ad boycott).
- **Ride existing arousal:** people already worked up (exciting shows, gyms, tense content, even exercise) share more — placement and timing near arousal peaks amplifies transmission; arousal also explains oversharing.

**Signature examples**
- *Denise Grady's "mysterious cough" article* — a fluid-dynamics science piece went viral on pure awe at a stunning schlieren photograph, despite low practical value.
- *Susan Boyle's Britain's Got Talent audition* — mockery turned to jaw-dropping awe; 100M+ views in nine days.
- *"United Breaks Guitars"* — musician Dave Carroll's revenge song about his smashed $3,500 guitar; millions of views in days, fueled by shared customer anger, alongside a ~10% ($180M) stock slide.
- *Google "Parisian Love"* — a love story told entirely in search queries turned the most functional product imaginable into an emotional hit the public demanded to share.
- *BMW "The Hire"* and *"Man Drinks Fat"* — high-arousal negative emotions (anxiety, disgust) driving massive transmission.

**Common mistakes**
- Leading with specs, statistics, and information dumps instead of a feeling.
- Choosing the wrong valence trap: making people *sad* (low arousal) when the campaign needed anger, awe, or humor — sad content gets sympathy, not shares.
- Settling for "pleasant/contented" reactions — comfortable satisfaction doesn't activate anyone to pass it on.

**Quick check**
- Name the single emotion this content evokes. Is it high-arousal (awe, excitement, amusement, anger, anxiety) or low-arousal (sadness, contentment)?
- Did we ask "why does this matter?" enough times to reach something human, or are we still describing features?
- If negative emotion is used, does it energize action for us — or could the anger turn on the brand?

## 4. Public

**What it is.** People imitate what they can *see* others doing ("monkey see, monkey do") — but they can only imitate the observable. Making products, ideas, and behaviors more visible makes them easier to copy and talk about. **Built to show, built to grow.**

**Why it works.** Others' choices are information — social proof resolves uncertainty (the crowded restaurant, the seeded tip jar, MBA students herding into banking, transplant patients wrongly refusing kidneys 99 others declined). But behavior is public while thoughts are private: Arizona students privately disliked binge drinking yet drank because everyone *saw* drinking and nobody saw the discomfort. Observability determines influence — car purchases influence neighbors more in sunny, drive-everywhere cities where cars are seen.

**Key mechanisms / tactics**
- **Make the private public:** turn invisible choices, opinions, and support into visible signals (Movember mustaches make cancer support wearable; "I Voted" stickers make a private act visible; publishing the true norm — most students drink moderately — cut binge drinking ~30%). Also useful for de-stigmatizing private topics (coining "ED," awareness days).
- **Self-advertising design:** build the product so using it promotes it — Hotmail's signature line, "Sent from my iPhone," Apple's flipped laptop logo (readable to onlookers, per Jobs's observability call), white iPod earbuds against a sea of black, Pringles cans, Louboutin red soles, prominent logos. Advertising without an ad budget.
- **Behavioral residue:** create physical traces that persist after use — Livestrong wristbands, Lululemon's sturdy reusable bags, Tiffany/Victoria's Secret bags people re-carry, trophies and race shirts, conference swag chosen for public use (mugs and gym bags beat makeup cases), Facebook Likes and auto-shared activity (Spotify) as digital residue.
- **The caution — don't publicize the wrong behavior:** visibility amplifies whatever it shows. Anti-drug PSAs *increased* teen marijuana use by broadcasting that lots of kids do drugs; "everyone steals petrified wood / pirates music" messages normalize the sin. To discourage a behavior, make it *less* visible or emphasize what people *should* do instead (Cialdini's Petrified Forest signs).

**Signature examples**
- *Movember* — mustaches turned invisible support for men's-health charity into a month-long walking billboard; from 30 friends to $174M+ raised worldwide.
- *Livestrong wristbands* — the yellow was chosen for visibility; 85M+ sold; chosen over a bike ride precisely because a wristband *persists* as daily residue.
- *Apple's laptop logo* — Jobs flipped it upside-down-to-the-owner so it reads right to everyone watching the laptop in use: observability beat owner convenience.
- *Hotmail's signature link* — every email advertised the service; 8.5M users in ~a year, sold to Microsoft for $400M.
- *Anti-drug ads that backfired* (and the kidney-list refusals) — social proof cuts both ways.

**Common mistakes**
- Hiding the product's use: consumption that happens privately (socks vs. shirts, toothpaste vs. cars) with no visible signal or residue designed in.
- Publicizing the prevalence of the *undesired* behavior ("so many people do X") when trying to stop X.
- Assuming private conviction spreads on its own — without visible signals, even a popular opinion stays trapped (everyone silent after the confusing presentation).

**Quick check**
- Can a bystander tell that someone is using/supporting this? What do observers see?
- Is there residue — something that keeps signaling after the moment of use or purchase?
- If the goal is to *reduce* a behavior, does the content accidentally show how common the bad behavior is?

## 5. Practical Value

**What it is.** People share useful things — news others can use — to help the people they care about. It's the modern barn raising: forwarding money-saving deals, health tips, and how-tos strengthens social bonds while genuinely helping. If Social Currency is about how the *sender* looks, Practical Value is about what the *receiver* gets. Sharing is caring.

**Why it works.** Helping others is intrinsically rewarding and socially bonding, and useful content gives an easy, low-cost way to do it at distance. In the NYT study, articles rated useful were ~30% more likely to be most-emailed (health, education, recipes dominate). Perceived value follows **prospect theory** (Kahneman & Tversky): people judge deals relative to **reference points**, not absolutes — the same grill sells better "reduced from $350 to $250" than "from $255 to $240"; just adding the word "sale" lifted catalog demand 50%+ at an unchanged price — and **diminishing sensitivity** means $10 off feels huge on a $35 clock radio and invisible on a $650 TV.

**Key mechanisms / tactics**
- **Highlight incredible value against expectations:** frame offers against a (higher) reference point; infomercial mechanics — anchor high, then reveal the price, then add bonus quantity ("but wait, there's more").
- **The Rule of 100:** under $100, percentage discounts look bigger (20% off a $25 shirt beats "$5 off"); over $100, absolute dollars look bigger ("$200 off" a $2,000 laptop beats "10% off"). Frame accordingly.
- **Scarcity/restriction sharpens deals:** limited-time offers, quantity limits ("3 per customer" can lift sales 50%+), and member-only pricing all signal "this must be good — act now." But permanent sales destroy the reference point (the always-70%-off carpet store).
- **Make savings visible** (ties to Public): loyalty-card savings buried on receipts spread nothing; visible or announced savings would.
- **Package usefulness for transmission:** short, tightly scoped, list-formatted content around one topic travels (Vanguard's one-page MoneyWhys newsletter; "5 ways to…" articles). Package your *expertise* so passing on the tip carries your brand along.
- **Narrow beats broad:** content for a niche audience is often *more* shared — it calls a specific person to mind ("my one friend who loves water polo") and creates an obligation to send it; broadly relevant content brings no one in particular to mind.
- **Truth caution:** the urge to help spreads falsehoods too (the fraudulent vaccine–autism claim kept circulating for years). Verify before amplifying; misinformation with practical-value framing travels as fast as truth.

**Signature examples**
- *Ken Craig's corn-shucking video* — an 86-year-old's two-minute microwave trick ("Clean Ears Everytime") hit 5M+ views, skewing to viewers 55+: pure usefulness, zero production value.
- *Hikers discussing vacuum cleaners* — deep in the wilderness with no triggers, no social currency, no emotion: practical value alone keeps people talking.
- *Vanguard's MoneyWhys* — a concise monthly finance-tips email that customers forward, spreading the firm's expertise with every send.
- *The grill / TV / clock-radio framing studies and the "sale" label experiment* — reference points and framing, not actual prices, drive perceived deals.
- *The vaccine scare* — practical-value sharing gone wrong; helpfulness spreads errors too.

**Common mistakes**
- Stating the offer without framing it: no reference point, wrong side of the Rule of 100, or a "discount" that has become the everyday expected price.
- Packaging usefulness badly — long, unfocused, many-topic content instead of one tight, forwardable unit.
- Chasing the broadest possible audience and producing generically relevant content that reminds nobody of anybody.

**Quick check**
- Would someone forward this to a *specific* person to help them? Who exactly?
- Is the value framed against a reference point, and is the discount expressed per the Rule of 100?
- Is the useful core easy to extract and pass on in one step (short, single-topic, listable)?

## 6. Stories

**What it is.** People don't think in information; they think in narratives — and while they focus on the story, information rides along inside it. Stories are **Trojan horses**: vessels that carry lessons, morals, and brand messages under the guise of idle chatter.

**Why it works.** Narrative has carried information for millennia (the Trojan Horse itself encodes "beware enemies bearing gifts"). Stories are how we learn culture cheaply — a friend's tale about an honest mechanic outperforms trial-and-error and advertising. They persuade where ads can't: you can't easily argue with someone's personal experience, and audiences absorbed in a plot lack the cognitive spare capacity to counter-argue. Stories also provide *psychological cover*: nobody walks up to a friend reciting "Subway has seven subs under six grams of fat," but everyone can tell the Jared story — and the proof rides along.

**Key mechanisms / tactics**
- **Build a Trojan horse:** wrap your message in a narrative people *want* to tell — with surprise, drama, or humor as the shell, and the brand's key takeaway as the cargo.
- **Make the product integral — "valuable virality":** the brand/benefit must be so woven into the plot that the story *cannot be told without it*. Test: retell the story in one sentence — does the brand survive? (You can't tell Will It Blend? without the blender; you can't explain the Panda ads without Panda cheese; but Evian's Roller Babies and the Golden Palace Olympics stunt are perfectly tellable — and were told — without the sponsor.)
- **Design for retelling:** stories get compressed as they travel — Allport & Postman's telephone-game studies show ~70% of details vanish within 5–6 retellings, and what survives is the critical plot core. Put your message in the core, not the trimmings, or it will be edited out.
- **Carry real lessons:** everyday stories (the Lands' End coat) smuggle multiple useful facts (which coats are warm, which company stands behind its products) inside an entertaining anecdote — reviews and recommendations naturally arrive as stories.
- **Stack STEPPS inside the story:** the best narratives are remarkable (Social Currency), emotional, and useful all at once — Jared, the $100 cheesesteak, Dove "Evolution."

**Signature examples**
- *Jared Fogle / Subway* — a man loses 245 pounds eating fast-food sandwiches: irresistible story, and Subway's health message is inseparable from it.
- *Dove "Evolution"* — time-lapse of makeup + Photoshop turning an ordinary woman into a billboard model; ignited a conversation about unreal beauty standards that carried Dove inside it. ~$135K to make, 16M+ views, tripled site traffic vs. its Super Bowl ad.
- *Blendtec "Will It Blend?"* — the durability benefit *is* the plot; ~$50 initial budget, 300M+ views, retail sales up 700%.
- *Panda cheese ads* — "Never say no to Panda": a panda trashes shops when people refuse the cheese; hilarious *and* brand-integral (counter-model: the same gag with a chicken suit would sell nothing).
- *Evian "Roller Babies" (cautionary)* — Guinness-record 50M+ views for the most-watched online ad, yet Evian lost share and sales fell ~25%: cute babies had nothing to do with the water. Likewise *Ron Bensimhon's Olympic pool dive* for GoldenPalace.com — everyone discussed the stunt, no one discussed the casino.

**Common mistakes**
- Buzz without benefit: a hilarious or shocking shell with the brand bolted on as a logo — shares accrue to the joke, not the sponsor.
- Message placed in trimmable details rather than the plot's spine, so retellings strip it out.
- Telling instead of storytelling: reciting claims and features the audience can (and will) counter-argue.

**Quick check**
- Retell the content in one sentence, as a friend would. Is the brand/message still in it?
- Is there an actual narrative arc (setup → tension → payoff) that people would repeat socially?
- Does the story's most repeatable moment coincide with the point we need remembered?

## Putting STEPPS to work

- **Checklist, not formula.** Berger is explicit: the six principles are toppings on a salad, not a strict recipe. Content doesn't need all six — but deliberately engineer several rather than hoping. Score any piece of content against each principle before shipping.
- **Complementary time horizons:** Social Currency and Emotion ignite *immediate* sharing; **Triggers sustain ongoing word of mouth** over weeks and months. Interesting gets the first conversation; cued gets the hundredth. Plan for both.
- **Two places to build STEPPS in:** in the product/idea itself (a phone-booth entrance, a yellow wristband, a song titled "Friday") or in the messaging around it (Will It Blend?, Kit Kat + coffee, MoneyWhys). Either works.
- **Some principles come easier per category** — nonprofits find Emotion natural; physical products find Public natural; almost anything can find Practical Value (the easiest principle to apply). The biggest wins often come from applying the principle that seems *hardest* for your category (social currency for a blender).
- **Anyone can do this.** No big budget, fame, or follower count required — the epilogue's Vietnamese nail-salon story shows ordinary people plus these principles building whole industries. Ordinary products, engineered to be talked about, are what actually catch on.

**Cross-cutting failure patterns to flag when reviewing content**
- **Viral but valueless:** huge attention that never mentions or requires the brand (Evian Roller Babies, the Olympic pool stunt). Always run the one-sentence-retell test.
- **Interesting but untriggered:** a clever one-off with no environmental cue to bring it back — expect a spike, then silence. Pair every launch with a recurring cue.
- **Informative but unfelt:** facts and features with no high-arousal emotion; correct, ignorable, unshared.
- **Persuasive but invisible:** private behavior with no public signal or residue, so it can't be imitated; or worse, visibility given to the behavior you're trying to stop.
- **Useful but unpackaged:** real value buried in long, multi-topic content with no reference-point framing — nobody can extract the forwardable piece.
- **Paid instead of social:** cash incentives replacing status, insider access, and genuine helpfulness — buzz that stops the moment payment stops.

**Evaluator's six questions (Berger's own checklist):**
1. **Social Currency** — does talking about it make people look good? Inner remarkability, game mechanics, insider status?
2. **Triggers** — what in the environment cues it, and how often? Can the habitat be grown?
3. **Emotion** — does it kindle a high-arousal feeling? When we care, we share.
4. **Public** — does it advertise itself? Does use leave behavioral residue others can see?
5. **Practical Value** — does sharing it help someone? Is the value highlighted, framed, and packaged for passing on?
6. **Stories** — what's the Trojan horse, and is the message inseparable from the tale?
